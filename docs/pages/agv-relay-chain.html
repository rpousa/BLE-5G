AGV Relay Chain

Three AGVs, one gNB on a pole. The AGV nearest the pole is the pivot: it talks to the gNB over NR (Uu) and relays for the others over the sidelink (PC5). The next one relays for the last, so the chain is gNB → pivot → relay → remote. Every AGV broadcasts its position on the sidelink, and as they drive the roles move from AGV to AGV.

Architecture

roles as drawn are one moment; the simulation below reassigns them as the AGVs move

Only the pivot uses the gNB's air time, so Uu carries all three AGVs' traffic through one UE. When the order of distances changes, the chain is rebuilt: the new pivot takes over the Uu traffic and the sidelink pairs are set up again. Planned boards: gNB board 3TJ2B15JGASV3 on the pole; AGV A on the UE1 board 2HOKMMVEX1WMP, AGV B on the UE2 board GBAMNH4BQ3CSB, AGV C on the BLE1 board 3VWLILYT2A1AK, reflashed to nrlink. Each board's LED shows its AGV's role in the colours used on this page: blue pivot, green relay, red remote.

On the boards, element by element

a UE per AGV, two attached · roles as at one moment: A pivot, B relay, C remote

Each AGV is one board and has its own OAI UE: A nrUE1, B nrUE2, C nrUE3. The two AGVs nearest the pole (pivot and relay) are in the gNB's coverage, and their UEs are attached. The fleet's traffic leaves through the pivot's UE. The relay's UE is a warm standby: it sends nothing, and the gNB still grants it a small block every TDD period, so its uplink stays ready (MCS 2, the configured maximum). On a pivot change, if the relay becomes pivot, its UE is already up and the traffic switches at once. Otherwise the old pivot's UE keeps carrying the traffic until the new pivot's UE has attached (make before break), and only then stops. The remote's UE is stopped, and its board answers the gNB's polls with nothing, as a UE that isn't there would. agv_chain.py on the UE PC plays the AGVs' computers. It sends hops only inside the gNB's windows, one board at a time, and each board's RGB LED shows its role: blue pivot, green relay, red remote. A pivot's LED blinks while its UE attaches. The numbers follow a positioning message from the remote AGV C to the edge server.

    Slot scheme and packet sequence

    as the boards ran it · timings from the recorded runs

    Slot scheme: OAI's slots, then a window for the sidelink

    The gNB side is the nFAPI split of the two-PC testbed: 30 kHz subcarriers, a TDD period of 10 slots (7 D, 1 S, 2 U), slowed down because the PNF waits for gnb_bridge to put each slot on the air. Empty D slots are not sent. With a UE per AGV the bridge polls all three; only the pivot's UE answers with its uplink (the other boards answer at once with nothing), and an uplink slot takes 12.6–14.1 ms. In the fleet runs it polled one UE, through whichever board was pivot (about 10.4 ms). After slot 9 of every period it broadcasts AIR_SLWIN and stays silent for 25 ms: the sidelink's air, granted by the gNB as a mode 1 resource pool would be. Inside the window the AGV boards send one at a time: hops, then position beacons every 0.5 s.

    At L2 the window carried about 5 packets (66 a second), 13 ms of its 17 usable ms on average; at L1, 3 packets in 6 ms. Paced, each packet also waits for the receivers' host transfer of the one before (≈ 10 µs a byte), which fills about 18 ms and leaves no room at peaks: about 150 messages a run waited past 250 ms and were dropped.

    • Radio packets on 2402 MHz between the four boards: the gNB's slots, polls and answers, the sidelink windows, the hops between AGVs, the position beacons.

    How it runs
    1. Four boards on one channel. The gNB board on the pole (core PC). AGV A on the UE1 board, B on the UE2 board, C on the BLE1 board, reflashed to nrlink v4 for the run (its Bluetooth firmware is backed up). All on 2402 MHz.
    2. The gNB grants the sidelink its air. After slot 9 of every TDD period, gnb_bridge announces a 25 ms window on the air and stays silent, as a mode 1 resource pool would. The chain transmits only inside those windows, one board at a time. The boards saw 13–15 windows a second: with the window, a TDD period lasted 67–77 ms.
    3. OAI's NR sidelink between the AGVs (option 3, the latest runs). The hops go over six OAI sidelink UEs, a pair for each pair of AGVs, carried by the boards in the gNB's windows; Uu as with two UEs attached. See "Option 3" below.
    4. Live on the testbed. The chain can run continuously as the testbed's live scenario (nrlink.sh live start --scenario chain on both PCs): option 3 at L1 with 25 ms windows. The core PC's live page (port 8090, on the LAN) then shows this page with a live card on top: the AGVs moving, the roles, every message's delay, and each board with the colour its LED shows. It was live on 12 Nov until the dual gateway took its place (--scenario dual).
    5. Two UEs attached (make before break). The pivot's and the relay's UEs are both attached; the fleet's traffic leaves through the nearest AGV whose session is up (the egress). If the relay becomes pivot, its UE is already up; otherwise the old pivot's UE carries on until the new one has attached, and only then stops. The standby UE sends nothing: the gNB grants every attached UE a small block each TDD period anyway (ulsch_max_frame_inactivity = 0), which keeps its uplink at MCS 2. A first pair of runs had the standby send 60 B every 0.2 s; that only cost uplink, see below.
    6. A UE per AGV, one attached. AGV A has nrUE1, B nrUE2 and C nrUE3, each over its own board, with its own IMSI and PDU session. Only the pivot's UE is in coverage. When the pivot changes, the old pivot's UE is stopped, and the gNB declares uplink failure and releases it. The new pivot's UE starts and attaches through its own board. Out-of-coverage boards answer their UE's polls with nothing, so the gNB polls three UEs and an uplink slot takes 12.6–14.1 ms instead of 10.4. Messages that reach the pivot before its session is up are dropped.
    7. One fleet UE (the first runs). A single nrUE held the fleet's session, and its radio moved to whichever board was pivot, so a pivot change cost nothing on Uu. This is easier on the numbers but not what a fleet would do; it shows the chain alone.
    8. Real hops between the AGVs. A relay's and a remote's messages cross the air board to board (up to 4 packets of 248 B), hop by hop, toward the pivot; commands go the other way. No retransmission on the hops, so a lost packet loses its message. A message that waits more than 250 ms for a window is dropped.
    9. Paced hops (all runs but the first two fleet ones). Every board hears every packet and spends about 10 µs a byte passing it to its PC before it is ready for the next one. In the paced run, the chain waits that long after each packet, plus 0.2 ms, before the next board sends.
    10. Positions on the air. Each board broadcasts its AGV's position every 0.5 s (every 100 ms in the model: the windows cannot carry that). The pivot applies the rule to the positions it heard, and each board's LED shows its role: blue pivot, green relay, red remote.
    What the boards say
    • Two UEs attached: pivot changes lose nothing at L1. Every flow lost 0–1.2 %, against about 19 % with one UE attached, and over 41 chain changes no message was dropped for want of a session. The delays are those of one UE attached (pivot positioning messages 133 ms at p50, relay 201 ms, remote 270 ms, against 130, 200, 270). The new UEs attached more slowly (median 4.9 s), but nothing waited for them.
    • The standby needs no keepalive. The first two-UE runs had the standby send 60 B every 0.2 s, to keep its uplink rate up. The gNB's statistics show that was never needed: every attached UE was at MCS 2, the configured maximum, within one statistics interval of attaching. With ulsch_max_frame_inactivity = 0 the gNB grants each attached UE a block every TDD period whether it sends or not. The keepalive only made the standby use its grants: in a 5-minute A/B at L2 it took 8.5 kbit/s of uplink against 2.9 kbit/s without, and the pivot's positioning messages took 1.26 s at p50 against 0.35 s. It is now off. At L1 the pivot's positioning messages dropped from 161 to 133 ms.
    • L2 hits two limits. Uplink: the pivot's own positioning messages never touch the sidelink, and spent 375 ms at p50 between the pivot's UE and the edge (129 ms at L1). The boards' uplink carried 62–65 kbit/s at most (80 at peaks) against about 60 kbit/s of L2 traffic with headers, so the standby's grants push it close to full. That ceiling is about half of what 106 PRBs at MCS 2 in 13 symbols would carry: grants averaged 190–280 B a slot. Whether OAI's grant sizing or the 1 Mbit/s board link sets it is not yet known. Sidelink: two UEs answering each uplink slot lengthen the TDD period, so the sidelink got 10.4 windows a second, against 12.0 with one UE attached. 3,263 messages aged out on the hops, and the remote lost 29 % of its positioning messages and 33 % of its commands.
    • With one UE attached, every pivot change is a real attach: median 3.3 s. All 57 attaches in those two runs succeeded. The container was up in 0.2 s, the UE synchronised on the SSB in 0.35 s and finished random access in 0.9 s. Registration and the PDU session took the remaining ~2.4 s, over the slowed slots. The 10th and 90th percentiles were 2.4 and 5.6 s, the worst 10.5 s. The gNB detected uplink failure on the UE that left after 10–12 missed PUSCH and released it about 1.3 s later.
    • The attaches cost about a fifth of the traffic. The pivot changed about 3 times a minute, so the fleet had no Uu for roughly 17 % of the time. At L1, about 19 % of every flow was lost (18.5–19.7 %), almost all of it dropped at the pivot for want of a session. Once a new UE had settled (8 s after its attach), pivot positioning messages took 119 ms at p50, as in the fleet runs. Over the whole run, relay and remote positioning messages took 200 and 270 ms.
    • A new UE starts slow. The gNB starts a new UE at uplink MCS 0 and raises it to the testbed's cap of 2 as its error rate stays low. L1 fits within the starting rate. L2 does not: after each attach the pivot's delay climbed for about 8 s, to 2.4 s, and took about 18 s to drain. With a pivot change every ~20 s, L2's positioning and LiDAR medians end at 1.2–1.7 s. A gNB that kept the link settings of a known UE, or a pivot that changes less often (more hysteresis), would avoid it.
    • The role order holds on real air. In every run of both designs, every flow is fastest for the pivot, slower for the relay and slowest for the remote. Each PC5 hop added 45–110 ms to a positioning message message, against 17–35 ms in the model: the boards got only 12–15 sidelink windows a second, so a hop mostly waits for the next window.
    • The pivot's own traffic matches Uu alone. Pivot commands took 57–64 ms at p50 in every run. Fleet-UE pivot positioning messages took 96–124 ms. Neither the sidelink windows nor the moving radio end of the fleet's session shows up in the pivot's numbers.
    • Unpaced hops lose small messages at L2. In the fleet runs without pacing, 10 % of the remote's positioning messages and 16 % of its commands were lost, almost all on the second hop. LiDAR scans barely lost anything. Pacing the hops by the receivers' host transfer time cut those losses to 1.5–1.8 %. It cost window capacity: remote delays rose by 65–95 ms, and about 150 messages waited past the 250 ms limit.
    • The roles changed 3–4 times a minute with 2 m of hysteresis, close to the model's rate. The board LEDs followed each change: blue pivot, green relay, red remote.

    Option 3: OAI's NR sidelink between the AGVs

    built and run on the boards · sidelink mode 2 · layer-3 relay · roles as at one moment: A pivot, B relay, C remote

    The hops between the AGVs go over OAI's NR sidelink: SDAP, PDCP, RLC and MAC, SCI 1-A and SCI 2 with each transport block, PSBCH for sync. Each pair of AGVs has a PC5 link made of two sidelink UEs from OAI's sidelink release, one on each AGV: a sync reference and a nearby UE. That is three links and six sidelink UEs, two per AGV. A hop is an IP packet over the link's tun. Each AGV's computer routes it on, toward the pivot or toward the AGV a command is for. As in step B, the PHY is replaced by nrlink_sl.c: the controller runs each UE's slots in real time and carries what it transmits between the AGV boards, inside the gNB's sidelink windows. All three links stay up, so a role change only re-routes; no sidelink UE restarts. Uu is option 2: a UE per AGV, the pivot's and the relay's attached. The numbers follow a positioning message from the remote AGV C to the edge server.

    Not yet as in the target design
    • Mode 2, not mode 1. The sidelink UEs choose their own resources. In mode 1 the gNB would grant them, which needs the sidelink UE attached to this gNB: OAI's sidelink release running Uu over the boards. That release and the boards' Uu code are in different OAI trees, and the gNB runs about 8× slower than real time over the boards while the sidelink runs in real time.
    • A layer-3 relay, not OAI's layer-2 relay. The AGVs' computers route IP packets, as in 3GPP's layer-3 UE-to-network relay. OAI's relay (SRAP, step C) is one hop only, and a chain needs two.
    • Links kept apart by link ids. The three links share one radio channel. They are separated by ids in the air header, not by frequency or by resource pool.
    • Beacons stay the controller's. The position beacons are still the controller's own packets.

    • L1 runs over OAI's sidelink. Every flow lost 1.6 % or less, and each sidelink hop 0.9 % or less (2,100–2,300 messages per hop direction). The chain changed 31 times without a sidelink UE restarting: the six UEs stayed in sync, and a role change only re-routed.
    • A sidelink hop costs more than the controller's packet. The sidelink part of a positioning message's delay was 79 ms for one hop (relay) and 195 ms for two (remote), against 54 and 122 ms with the controller's packets. The extra comes from the sidelink UE's own slot timing (it sends when its MAC has a resource) on top of the wait for the gNB's window. The Uu part was the same in both: 123–130 ms.
    • L2 does not fit in the windows. OAI's sidelink puts about 7.6 kB/s of items on the air at L2 (transport blocks with their SCI, PSBCH every few slots, MAC, RLC, PDCP and IP headers). The windows were full (20.7 ms of each 25 ms window in use), and items waited in the UEs' outboxes until they were too old: 38–59 % of the relay's and remote's messages were lost. The controller's hops also lost up to 33 % at L2. The pivot lost 9 % because, while a new pivot's UE settles, its own messages hop over the sidelink to the UE carrying the traffic.
    • Wider windows move the limit to the Uu uplink. With 50 ms windows instead of 25 the sidelink carries L2: each hop lost 0.2–2.5 % (22–62 % before), and a command reaches the remote in 354 ms with 1.9 % lost. But the gNB stays off the air longer each frame: frames fell from 11.2 to 8.1 a second, and the uplink slots with them, to about 45 kbit/s, below what the three AGVs send at L2. The pivot's UE queued the whole fleet's uplink: every positioning waited about 6.5 s, whatever the AGV's role, and 21–22 % aged out. 35 ms lands in between: 9.4 frames a second leave the uplink just enough (the pivot's positioning messages 385 ms, 3.4 % lost, no multi-second queue), but the sidelink, with 29 of its 35 ms in use, still lost 9–34 % per hop, so the relay lost 21 % and the remote 31 %. No width carries L2 on these boards: the sidelink and the uplink share one channel, and L2 needs more than its total. L1 stays on 25 ms.
    • Two fixes from these runs. A UE that had just attached sometimes queued for 3–7 s when it took the traffic straight away. It now waits 8 s while the other attached UE carries on (--egress-settle); this also happened, less often, in option 2. And the position beacons now go before the sidelink items in each window: in the first L2 run the sidelink filled every window, no beacon got through, and the chain stopped following the AGVs.
